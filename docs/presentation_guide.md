## Outline (6 minutes)

### 1. The question (0:00–0:45)
> "Why does sugar dissolve in water but oil doesn't? And could a computer *learn* the answer
> just from data?"

* Solubility matters everywhere: medicines must dissolve to work, pesticides wash into rivers,
  and cooking depends on it.
* We measure it as **logS**, the log of mol/L. Each step of 1 is a factor of 10.

### 2. The chemistry in one slide (0:45–1:45)
Show the **Chemistry corner** page.
* **Like dissolves like:** water is polar, so polar molecules dissolve and oily ones don't (logP).
* **Hydrogen bonds:** –OH, –NH₂ and –COOH groups bond with water.
* **Size:** big molecules need a big hole in water.
* **Crystal:** tightly packed solids resist dissolving (melting point).

### 3. How the model works (1:45–3:00)
* **Data:** 8,754 real lab measurements (AqSolDB), after removing salts, inorganics and conflicting duplicates.
* **Molecule → numbers:** 14 descriptors a chemist knows (logP, size, polar surface, H-bond donors…),
  plus a 1,024-bit "fingerprint" of small fragments.
* **Model:** gradient boosting, 400 small decision trees, each one fixing the errors of the ones before it.
* **Honest testing:** 20% of molecules hidden until the end. Two exams:
  * *random split*: test molecules have "cousins" in training (easy);
  * *scaffold split*: test molecules have **new ring skeletons** (hard, more realistic).

### 4. Live demo (3:00–5:00)
On the **Predict** page:
1. **Ethanol:** highly soluble. Point at the blue O–H atom and read the sentence.
2. **1-Octanol:** much less soluble. Same –OH group, but the long carbon chain turns red.
3. **Glucose:** five –OH groups, all blue. Very soluble even though it is bigger than octanol.
4. **Benzene vs phenol:** a single –OH group raises logS by about 1.8, so phenol is about 60× more soluble
   (the measured difference is about 1.6, roughly 40×).
5. **Caffeine / aspirin:** everyday molecules. Mention the 90% range.
6. **An "unfamiliar" molecule:** type `C12C3C4C1C5C2C3C45` (**cubane**, a strained cube of 8 carbons).
   The closest training molecule is only 22% similar, so the orange "outside the model's experience"
   warning appears and the range is wider. Use it to make the point that a good model also says when it is unsure.

On the **Compare** page: click the **Alcohol chain** preset (ethanol → 1-butanol → 1-octanol → hexane)
and point at the downward staircase in the trend chart.

### 5. Results and honesty (5:00–5:45)
On **How the model works**:
* Typical error **0.84 log units** on the random split and **1.01** on new skeletons. That's within a
  factor of ~7–10, across a range that spans a factor of 10¹⁵.
* **Human vs machine:** Delaney's 2004 formula has an error of 1.35. The model cuts that by 30–40%.
* **It learned real chemistry:** the logP and polar-surface charts slope the way chemistry predicts.
* **Limits:** no temperature, no pH, no crystal structure; salts are excluded. Several "errors" are
  actually wrong measurements in the dataset.

### 6. Conclusion (5:45–6:00)
> "A computer, given only structures and measurements, rediscovered 'like dissolves like',
> hydrogen bonding and size effects. It still can't see crystals, and it tells you when it's unsure."

## Likely questions and answers

**Q: Is this ChatGPT / an AI chatbot?**
No. It's a regression model (LightGBM) trained on 8,754 measured solubilities. It only turns
numbers about a molecule into one number (logS). Nothing is generated by a language model.

**Q: How accurate is it?**
On molecules it never saw, the typical error (RMSE) is 0.84 log units, about a factor of 7. For
completely new ring skeletons it is 1.01. R² is 0.87 and 0.79. For comparison, the measurements
themselves often disagree by ~0.5 log units between labs.

**Q: What's the difference between the random and scaffold split?**
Random: hide random molecules, which usually have close relatives in training. Scaffold: hide whole
families with the same ring skeleton. Scaffold is harder and shows how well the model handles
genuinely new chemistry.

**Q: Why is logP so important?**
logP measures how much a molecule prefers oil over water, which is basically "like dissolves like"
in one number. Delaney's 2004 formula is mostly logP too.

**Q: Why not just use logP then?**
Delaney's formula (logP + 3 other variables) has an error of 1.35. The model adds hydrogen bonding,
specific groups and aromatic structure and gets 0.84.

**Q: How do the atom colours work?**
SHAP measures how much each feature pushed this prediction. Each push is shared among the atoms that
cause it: a fragment's atoms, each atom's share of logP, the O and N atoms that hydrogen-bond. Blue
atoms push solubility up and red atoms push it down.

**Q: Why can't it predict NaCl?**
Salts dissolve by splitting into ions. That depends on lattice energy and ion hydration, which the
model's organic features can't describe. We removed salts from the training data on purpose, and the
website explains this instead of giving a wrong number.

**Q: What does the 90% range mean?**
We looked at the model's errors on molecules it hadn't seen and made the range wide enough to contain
90% of them. On the test set it actually contained 87–89%, slightly under target, and I report that.

**Q: When does it fail?**
Mostly for flat, tightly packed molecules like anthraquinone dyes, which it predicts too soluble
because it can't see the crystal. Some big "errors" are actually bad data: for example, a siloxane
listed as highly soluble in the dataset.

**Q: Did you check you didn't cheat?**
Yes. The test molecules were hidden during training and tuning, models were chosen by
cross-validation only, everything uses a fixed random seed, and one command reproduces all results.
I also found that ESOL is almost entirely contained in AqSolDB, so I did *not* use ESOL as an
"independent" test of the AqSolDB model.

**Q: Why gradient boosting and not a neural network?**
For a dataset of about 9,000 molecules with good descriptors, tree models are a strong, standard
choice, and they are much easier to explain. Neural networks might gain a little accuracy, but
explaining *why* was a main goal of this project.

**Q: Does temperature matter?**
Yes, a lot, but all the data are at about 25 °C, so the model can't predict other temperatures.
